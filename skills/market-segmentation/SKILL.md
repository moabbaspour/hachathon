# Market Segmentation and Sizing

## Mission
Help a team decide whom to serve first, estimate the opportunity with transparent assumptions, and find a credible route to early demand and eventual product-market fit. TAM is context; a narrow, reachable SOM and evidence of demand guide the first experiment.

## Important scope and accuracy rules
- Do not present market size as customer validation. A large TAM does not prove urgency, willingness to pay, or a route to customers.
- Distinguish consumer and B2B models. ACV × number of organizations fits a suitable recurring/contract model; consumer, transaction, usage, or impact models need the right unit economics and denominator.
- Use dated, traceable sources for counts, prices, ACV, buying cycles, and conversion rates. Give formulas, ranges, inclusion/exclusion rules, and confidence.
- The workshop’s U.S. education example, fit/friction scores, and “95:5” illustration are not universal facts. Do not reuse its sample figures or assume its ratios apply. Verify domain-specific benchmarks or label them as scenario assumptions.
- Keep market boundaries consistent: segment definition, geography, buyer, product capability, period, and pricing unit must align.
- Avoid false precision. Use ranges when inputs are estimates; show sensitivity to the largest assumptions.
- Do not add overlapping subsegments together unless you have deduplicated entities.

## Workflow

### 1. Define product and economic unit
Write the product/service, problem, buyer, pricing model, annual contract value (or average revenue per customer/transaction), and the period. For a hackathon concept without a validated price, mark price as an assumption and use scenarios.

### 2. Segment the market
Create mutually exclusive or explicitly overlapping candidate segments using relevant attributes:
- geographic: city, region, country, operating radius, language;
- firmographic: industry, organization size, revenue, employee count;
- technographic: current systems, devices, infrastructure, cloud/on-premise;
- contextual/behavioral: workflow, trigger, usage pattern, urgency;
- buyer role: user, decision-maker, budget owner.

A segment must be findable in the real world and specific enough to recruit interviewees or enumerate accounts.

### 3. Calculate TAM
TAM is the theoretical total revenue opportunity in the clearly defined target category under an explicit model; it is not a forecast, immediate target, or amount the startup will capture. Build it bottom-up where possible:

> TAM = eligible units in defined market × annual revenue per unit

List subsegments, unit counts, source/date, ACV or price, inclusion rules, and whether categories overlap. Reconcile the total and show a low/base/high range. A top-down estimate may be a cross-check, not a substitute for explainable unit math.

### 4. Calculate SAM
SAM is the part of TAM the current product, business model, geography, regulation, language, integrations, and delivery model can actually serve. Filter to a small number of plausible verticals (the workshop suggests 2–4) and justify each exclusion. Use early positive conversations, active use, beta adoption, low churn, requests, or existing paid demand if available; otherwise narrow with explicit capability/access constraints rather than inventing traction.

### 5. Compare fit and friction
Score each candidate using evidence, not intuition alone. Keep separate columns:

**Fit (higher is better):** access/geography, customer-size alignment, integration fit, industry/workflow knowledge, evidence of pain, ability to deliver value.

**Friction (lower is better):** sales-cycle complexity and length, regulation, security, acquisition cost, service cost, onboarding, competition, customization, and other segment-specific blockers.

Use a 1–10 scale only with anchored definitions (what a 1, 5, and 10 mean). Record confidence and evidence per score. Averages can help compare but must not conceal a fatal blocker or low-confidence score. Map segments on fit versus friction and investigate the most promising reachable beachhead.

### 6. Define SOM and beachhead
SOM is the realistically obtainable short-term portion of SAM given team capacity, delivery, access, sales cycle, competition, and time. Narrow to 1–2 initial segments and an actionable list: geography, org/user size, buyer, department/workflow, eligibility, and estimated account count. Estimate near-term revenue using a feasible penetration/capacity assumption, not 100% capture of the segment.

Use bottom-up capacity checks: how many prospects can the team contact, onboard, serve, and support during the period? How many buying cycles fit? What evidence supports each conversion assumption? The SOM should be something the team can name and test.

### 7. Estimate in-market demand carefully
If analyzing recurring purchase cycles, a rough timing estimate can be:

> buyers entering a buying window during period P ≈ eligible accounts N × P / average purchase cycle T

Use only when purchases are reasonably distributed and T is supported. Seasonality, renewals, budgets, incidents, and nonuniform triggers can invalidate this approximation. Then model funnel stages separately (reachable → engaged → qualified → opportunity → won) with sourced or explicitly hypothetical conversion rates. Do not treat a funnel arithmetic result as observed demand.

### 8. Choose route to PMF
Use **problem-first / wider exploration** when ICP is genuinely unknown: lead with a validated problem, run comparable tests across a few reachable groups, measure response, and let a segment emerge. Use **beachhead-first** when evidence suggests a promising segment: define ICP and decision-makers, test focused positioning and delivery, and track paid/retained use. In either route, state the learning goal and stop/continue criteria. Expand to adjacent segments only after evidence in the first segment.

### 9. Validate fastest route
For each candidate segment, design a low-cost test: recruit target users/buyers, test past behavior and current workarounds, show a prototype only after problem exploration, record conversion behavior, time-to-decision, and delivery cost. “Positive conversations” are signals, not PMF. Track retention/repeat use for recurring products and repeat purchase/transaction economics where appropriate. No retention evidence means PMF remains unproven.

## Required outputs
1. Market definition and economic unit.
2. Segmentation tree and account/user counting method.
3. TAM/SAM/SOM model with cited inputs, formulas, ranges, and assumptions.
4. Fit/friction matrix with anchored scores, sources, confidence, and fatal blockers.
5. Selected beachhead and named reachable prospect/user profile.
6. Near-term demand model and funnel with assumptions clearly separated from observed data.
7. Fastest validation experiment, success/failure criteria, and expansion logic.
8. Pitch-ready market slide: one sentence each for TAM, SAM, SOM; methodology and caveat in speaker notes.

## Pitch quality check
Before presenting, verify that the three market numbers use consistent geography, customer definition, period, and revenue unit; the SOM is reachable; every source and assumption is visible; funnel math is not represented as observed demand; and the chosen segment links directly to the MVP test. If the numbers are weak, present a transparent range and explain the validation plan.
