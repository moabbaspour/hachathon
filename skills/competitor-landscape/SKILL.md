# Competitor Landscape

## Mission
Find everyone who has tried to solve this problem, or who customers use instead, and learn from them with evidence. Explain why the failures failed and why the successes succeeded. Show how big each player is, what it actually delivered, and how much money it raised. The goal is to find the gap that is still open and the mistakes not to repeat. Claiming "we have no competitors" is not an acceptable result.

## Evidence rules
- **Five-year window.** Each player's current status, scale, and funding must come from sources published within the five years before the run date. Older events, such as a shutdown in an earlier year, may be included as history, labelled with their date.
- **Every fact has a dated source.** A fact without one is labelled unverified. Prefer primary sources: company filings, registries, official announcements, investor announcements, and court or regulatory records. Then come reputable business press and funding databases such as Crunchbase, Dealroom, PitchBook, and Tracxn. Note when a database is paywalled and you saw only a summary.
- **Separate claims from facts.** A company's own claims (users, revenue, impact) are marked as claims unless an independent source confirms them.
- **Funding figures** need the date, round stage, amount, currency, lead investors, and source. Record "not disclosed" rather than estimating. Don't convert currencies without giving the rate and its date.
- **Failure causes** need evidence: a post-mortem, a founder interview, reporting, or a filing. Label each cause as **stated** (by the company or founders) or **inferred** (your analysis). Never invent a reason.
- **No research tools, no data.** If you can't search, say so. List the players and questions to check, and mark every field unverified.

## Workflow

### 1. Define the field
Use the problem statement and geography from the brief. Look for players in five groups:
- **Direct competitors**: same problem, same users.
- **Indirect competitors**: same problem, different approach or users.
- **Substitutes and workarounds**: spreadsheets, intermediaries, informal services, or doing nothing.
- **Public and non-profit efforts**: government programmes, NGOs, donor-funded pilots.
- **Big adjacent players** that could enter.

Search locally first, then regionally, then globally. Pay attention to players that solved the same problem in a similar geography.

### 2. Profile each player
Record:
- name, country, founded, and current status: active, acquired, shut down, or pivoted, with the date;
- what it offers, its business model, and its target users;
- **scale**: users or customers, revenue if known, employees, geographies served, and growth signals, each with a source and date;
- **delivered projects**: notable deployments, clients, pilots, and measured outcomes, with evidence;
- **funding**: total raised and every round (date, stage, amount, currency, lead investors, source), plus grants, acquisitions, and exits;
- strengths, weaknesses, and pricing where known.

### 3. Explain failures
For every player that shut down, shrank, pivoted away, or stalled, find out why. Classify each cause, giving evidence and stated/inferred for each:
- no real need;
- wrong customer or payer;
- unit economics;
- distribution or customer acquisition;
- regulation;
- timing (too early or too late);
- technology;
- trust or adoption;
- funding environment;
- team or execution;
- competition.

Then ask whether the same cause still applies today in this geography, and what has changed since.

### 4. Explain successes
For every player that grew, raised follow-on rounds, or proved impact, identify what drove it, with evidence: distribution channel, partnerships, regulation, pricing, product insight, timing, or funding. Separate what can be copied from what depended on unique conditions.

### 5. Find the gap
Plot the players on the two or three dimensions that matter most to users, for example price against depth of service, or reach against accuracy. Name the segments, needs, or geographies nobody serves well, and explain why: whether the gap is an opportunity or a trap others already fell into. Pass the gaps, failure causes, and success drivers to the `solution-selection` skill.

## Required outputs
1. Competitor table: name | group | country | status | offer | model | scale indicators (with dates) | key projects | strengths | weaknesses | sources.
2. Funding table: player | round | date | amount and currency | lead investors | source | verified or claimed.
3. Failure analysis: player | what happened | causes (stated or inferred) | evidence | whether it still applies here.
4. Success analysis: player | drivers | evidence | whether it can be copied.
5. Positioning map and a description of the open gaps.
6. Lessons: the mistakes the team must avoid, and the advantages it can borrow.
7. Pitch competition slide: positioning, why we differ, and why the failures don't doom us, each line with a source.

## Quality check
Before handing off, confirm that:
- every funding figure and scale number has a dated source and the claimed/verified flag;
- every failure cause is backed by evidence and marked stated or inferred;
- the substitutes and the status quo are included;
- nothing relies on data older than five years except labelled history;
- the gaps follow from the evidence, not from wishful thinking.
