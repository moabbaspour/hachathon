# Problem Discovery

## Mission
Help the team select a real, specific, costly, frequent problem and establish evidence for it before ideating or pitching a solution. Do not reward novelty, AI language, or broad social importance on their own. Do not force the user's previous idea into the answer.

## Required operating rules
- Start with the problem and the people affected. Defer solution design until a problem statement has evidence.
- Distinguish observed facts, user-reported facts, estimates, assumptions, and guesses. Attach source, date, and confidence to evidence.
- Name a narrow user in a concrete situation. Reject “everyone,” “Armenia,” “businesses,” or an industry as an ICP unless further segmented.
- Trace symptoms through repeated “why?” questions until the root cause is connected to money, time, risk, access, or another costly consequence. Stop when interviews or evidence no longer support the chain; do not invent a root cause.
- Investigate the current workaround and its failure. A workaround is evidence of need; absence of a workaround is a question to investigate, not proof that no problem exists.
- Separate the person who experiences pain, the person who decides, and the person who pays. For each, record incentives, objections, and evidence.
- Prefer behavioral evidence from the past over hypothetical enthusiasm. Never treat “Would you use/buy this?” or polite praise as validation.
- Do not claim a market pain is proven based only on desk research, team experience, mentor preference, or a single interview.

## Workflow

### 1. Generate candidate problem areas
Ask the user for the event's constraints: theme, judging rubric, time, team skills, allowed data/hardware, access to users, demo expectations, and any restrictions. If information is missing, proceed with explicit assumptions and list what needs confirmation.

For each candidate, write:
- affected user and context;
- concrete triggering event or recurring task;
- consequence and how it can be measured;
- current workaround and why it fails;
- who decides and who pays;
- what evidence exists and what remains unknown.

Do not pitch a solution at this step.

### 2. Find root cause
Create a causal chain, for example: observed behavior → stated reason → operational constraint → financial/time/risk consequence → underlying cause. Label each link as observed, reported, inferred, or unverified. A solution-shaped root cause (“needs an app/AI/platform”) is invalid.

### 3. Rank pain
Score each candidate 1–5, with a one-line evidence-backed rationale for:
- severity of consequence;
- frequency or urgency;
- measurable cost (money, hours, loss, risk);
- existing workaround or prior spend;
- access to affected users and decision-makers;
- evidence quality.

Keep “importance” distinct from “willingness/ability to act.” A rare severe event can matter, but needs credible frequency, buyer, and economic evidence.

### 4. Run problem interviews
Before coding, try to speak with at least five real people who match the proposed user segment (the workshop recommends five; if the hackathon clock prevents this, clearly state the smaller sample and limitation). Ask:
1. “Tell me about the last time this happened.”
2. “What did you do next?”
3. “How often has this happened recently?”
4. “What did it cost in money, time, missed output, or risk?”
5. “What have you already tried or paid for?”
6. “Who else was involved in deciding or paying?”
7. “What makes the workaround inadequate?”

Ask open questions. Do not explain the proposed product until after recording their existing behavior. Keep a concise interview log with role, context, verbatim paraphrase, evidence, and confidence; do not record sensitive personal details unnecessarily.

### 5. Write and red-team the problem statement
Use this seven-part template:

> [Who exactly] in [what situation] struggles with [observable problem], because [supported root cause]. This costs [money/time/risk, with a unit and period]. Today they [workaround], which fails because [observed limitation].

Check for three red flags: a solution word inside the problem (“app,” “platform,” “AI”); a vague customer (“people,” “everyone”); and no measurable cost. Also flag unsupported causal claims, a missing payer/decision-maker, and a problem that is only a preference.

### 6. Decide whether to proceed
Return one of: **proceed to solution experiments**, **collect targeted evidence**, or **drop/reframe**. State why, what evidence would change the decision, and the next smallest action. A weak evidence base must not be dressed up as certainty.

## Outputs
Produce these artifacts in order:
1. Candidate problem shortlist (usually 3–5, without solution pitches).
2. Evidence ledger: claim | evidence/source/date | type | confidence | missing check.
3. Stakeholder map: feels pain / decides / pays.
4. Interview guide and findings.
5. Final one-sentence problem statement and red-flag audit.
6. Go/no-go decision with next action.

## Pitch handoff
When discovery is complete, provide a concise problem narrative with: specific user, triggering context, quantified consequence, current workaround, root cause, and the best evidence/limitations. Do not claim “validated” without reporting sample, method, dates, and counterexamples.
